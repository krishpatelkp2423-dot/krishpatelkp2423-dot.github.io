Modified Chess with a Reinforcement-Learning AI

Chess without checkmate. Every capture scores points and the first player to 15 wins. I built the game in Python, and I'm training a reinforcement-learning agent on the new rules to compare how a machine and a person adapt to the same unfamiliar incentives.

1The new rules

Traditional chess revolves around checkmate, which narrows strategy and makes the game hard to pick up. I wanted to see what happens to strategy when the win condition changes. In this version:

  • Every captured piece adds its point value to the capturer's score.
  • No check, no checkmate. The king can be captured without ending the game.
  • The first player to reach 15 points wins, shown on a live side-panel scoreboard.

Each side starts with 41 points of material, so winning means taking a little more than a third of your opponent's army. The queen alone is worth 60% of a win.

3Humans vs. the AI: the psychology of new rules

The most interesting part of this project is psychological. A human player sits down with years of chess instinct built around protecting the king and hunting for checkmate. The agent sits down with nothing but the rules and a reward. Changing the win condition lets me see which instincts help, which ones get in the way, and how fast each kind of player adjusts.

Playtesting so far shows player strategies differ significantly from standard chess. The comparison with the trained agent is built around these questions:

Human player
RL agent
Starting point

Arrives with checkmate habits, opening theory, and a feel for "good" positions that was learned under different rules.

Starting point

Starts from zero. It knows only the legal moves and what earns reward, so it has no old habits to unlearn.

The king

Instinct says guard the king at all costs, even though here it's worth only 2 points and losing it doesn't end the game. How long does that instinct last?

The king

Values the king only as much as the reward does. Does it learn to treat the king as an ordinary 2-point piece, or even use it as an attacker?

Risk and the queen

The queen is 9 of the 15 points. Losing it hands the opponent 60% of a win. Do players become more cautious with big pieces, or chase the quick 9?

Risk and the queen

Learns the price of risk only from outcomes. Does it trade more aggressively, or more conservatively, than people do?

Thinking in points

Has to switch from "trap the king" to counting the score every move. A player at 12 points needs just one knight or bishop to win.

Thinking in points

Optimizes the points race from its first training game, since that's the only thing the reward measures.

Learning speed

Can read the rule sheet once and reason about it right away.

Learning speed

Needs many training games to discover what a person picks up from reading the rules. How many games until it plays at a human level?

4What I built

Game development

Designed and implemented the board, piece movement, rule enforcement, and user interaction in Python with Pygame.

Rule-system redesign

Removed the checkmate logic and replaced it with point tracking, which meant making the rule handling modular enough to change the win condition cleanly.

User interface

A side-panel scoreboard tracks both players' points in real time and declares the winner the moment someone reaches 15.

AI & reinforcement learning

Training an agent with Q-learning / policy-based methods and function approximation to learn the new rules from scratch and play against people.
