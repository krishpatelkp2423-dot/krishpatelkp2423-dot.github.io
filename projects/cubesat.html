2The FlatSat

A FlatSat is every piece of hardware on our satellite, mounted flat on a rack instead of stacked inside the 2U structure. With everything spread out, every board can be probed, rewired, and swapped while we integrate and test.

I'm in charge of ours. When a subteam hands me hardware, I integrate it onto the rack and connect it to the rest of the system, especially the power wiring. Then I watch the current draws to make sure the power system can supply every subteam at once.

What I check on every integration
  • Power wiring matches the interface documents
  • Each subsystem's current draw under power
  • Total draw stays inside the power budget
  • Hardware-software interfaces behave as specified

5What I do

FlatSat & integration

In charge of the FlatSat. I integrate the hardware each subteam gives me, handle the power wiring between boards, watch current draws, and run the system integration test plans that check hardware-software interfaces against program-level dispenser and safety requirements.

Power

Work with the power subteam in the lab on an in-house, aerospace-grade battery, making sure it meets its requirements, and work with every other subteam to make sure each one gets the power it needs.

Architecture & documentation

Develop and maintain CONOPS diagrams, Interface Control Documents, and software topology requirements so every subteam builds to the same interfaces.

Mission planning & risk

Model orbital data and simulate mission stages in Systems Tool Kit (STK) to validate orbits and communication windows. Track mass, power, and link budgets, and mission risks through FMEA.

6Next milestone: a balloon test flight

Before the satellite goes to orbit, the team is flying its electronics on a weather balloon. We call it a high-altitude FlatSat: the real power board, flight computer, radio, sensors, wiring harness and flight software ride to about 32 km, run on their own in air colder than −60 °C at about 1% of sea-level pressure, and come back down under a parachute. The target launch date is April 17, 2027.

My part: I own the requirements for this flight. The mission has 38 requirements and 15 test cases. I keep them in the team's master requirements sheet and track how each one gets verified, by test, analysis or inspection.

What the flight can and cannot prove

It can proveIt cannot prove
The power board, flight computer, radio, sensors and harness working together on battery power for hoursSolar charging and the power balance in orbit
Flight software running on its own: modes, logging, telemetry, and recovery from faultsAttitude control, because a payload hanging on a line cannot be steered
A radio link over more than 100 km, with a GPS position in every packetVibration and shock from a rocket launch
Operation in very cold, thin air, where parts lose their air coolingThermal cycling, vacuum and radiation over months in orbit

Requirements I track

IDThe payload shallWhyChecked by
HR-01Run the whole mission, from power-on to recovery, without any ground commandThe whole bus is tested as one systemTest
HR-02Run at least 8 hours on one battery charge, heater includedAbout 3.5 hours powered, plus holds and recoveryTest
HR-03Weigh no more than 2.72 kg (6 lb) packedKeeps the flight inside the FAA's rules for light balloonsInspection
HR-11Detect launch, burst and landing by itself and change mode within 60 secondsThe system has to run with no human helpTest
HR-12After any reset, be back in the correct mode and transmitting within 60 secondsThe flight computer has to recover from faults by itselfTest

Five of the 38. A flight counts as a full success if telemetry is received for at least 90% of it, the onboard log is at least 95% complete, every reset recovers automatically, and the payload is recovered within 24 hours.

Power budget for the flight

LoadAverage power
Flight computer0.50 W
Power board electronics and regulators0.30 W
Radio, transmitting 8% of the time0.26 W
Sensors and SD card logging0.20 W
Comms microcontroller and radio listening0.15 W
GPS0.10 W
Total, at about 85% converter efficiency≈ 1.8 W

The team plans around 2.0 W. Over the 8 hours the battery has to last, that is 16 Wh. The battery heater adds 12 Wh and the magnetorquer driver tests add 0.5 Wh, for about 28.5 Wh in total.

The battery is a custom pack of six 18650 cells that stores about 72 Wh. That is roughly 2.5 times what the flight needs, or 2 times if the cold costs a fifth of its capacity.

These are the team's estimates from the mission's concept of operations (Rev C, November 2026). They get replaced with measured values in the endurance test.

7System block diagram

To integrate hardware on the FlatSat I need to know where every wire goes. I put together this block diagram of the whole satellite from each subteam's signal list. It shows 75 connections, one line per wire: the four solar panels and the battery into the power board, the power rails out to every load, and the signals that cross between the three boards in the stack.
