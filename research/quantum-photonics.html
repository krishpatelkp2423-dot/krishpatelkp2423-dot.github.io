Integrated Quantum Photonics

Trapped-ion quantum computers are controlled with laser light, and today that light usually reaches the ions through a table full of free-space optics. My research looks at moving those optics onto the chip itself, with waveguides, grating couplers, and modulators that deliver light straight to each ion.

1Why put the optics on a chip

Today: free-space optics

Each trapped ion is a qubit, and lasers cool it, set its state, run gates on it, and read it out. In most labs those beams reach the ions through lenses, mirrors, and modulators spread across an optical table. The setups are large, drift out of alignment, and get harder to manage with every ion you add.

The goal: integrated photonics

Build the optics into the microfabricated trap. Waveguides carry the light across the chip and grating couplers send it up to each ion. Because fabrication fixes the light paths, they stay aligned, and one chip can carry many beams to many ions. The result is more compact, more stable qubit control that can scale.

4What I analyzed

I analyzed how well grating couplers and waveguide structures deliver photons to trapped-ion qubits, judged on three parameters.

Parameter 1

Coupling efficiency

How much of the light in the waveguide actually leaves the grating in the intended beam. Light lost here is wasted laser power, and stray light can reach ions it wasn't meant for.

Parameter 2

Mode overlap

How closely the shape of the emitted beam matches the beam profile needed at the ion. Better overlap means a cleaner, better-focused spot on the qubit.

Parameter 3

Fabrication compatibility

Whether the photonic layers can be made in the same process as the trap's electrodes, without one fabrication step damaging the other.

5What's next

This project is wrapping up, so the lab is choosing its next direction. I'm helping by reading papers and finding new ideas to explore in two areas: photonic chips for quantum computing, and photonics for AI datacenters.

Two families of quantum computers. Gate-based machines run logic gates on physical qubits, such as superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and neutral atoms. Measurement-based or fusion-based machines instead compute by measuring large entangled states, and photonic versions build those states from photons made by sources such as SPDC and SFWM. Each approach has its own strengths and limits, and photonic integrated circuits (PICs) matter to both.

Quantum computing

  1. PICs for neutral atomsPhotonic circuits that deliver light to arrays of neutral atoms, another leading qubit platform.Google is working on this platform · Starting paper: arXiv:2606.07800
  2. PICs for trapped ionsOn-chip light delivery for trapped-ion qubits, the area my current project sits in.IonQ, Quantinuum · Related publication from a lab colleague
  3. SPDC-based PICsPhoton-pair sources based on spontaneous parametric down-conversion, where one pump photon splits into two.Xanadu
  4. SFWM-based PICsPhoton-pair sources based on spontaneous four-wave mixing, where two pump photons turn into a new pair.PsiQuantum
  5. Integrated quantum photonicsThe wider field of generating, routing, and measuring quantum states of light on a chip.

AI & datacenter

  1. Optical interconnectMoving data between chips, boards, and racks with light instead of copper.
  2. PICs for LPOLinear Pluggable Optics: pluggable transceivers that drop the power-hungry DSP and drive the optics directly.
  3. PICs for NPONear-Packaged Optics: optical engines mounted on the board right next to the switch or processor.
  4. PICs for CPOCo-Packaged Optics: optical engines inside the same package as the switch or processor, for the shortest electrical path.
  5. Photonic AI acceleratorsChips that use light to do the matrix math behind neural networks.
