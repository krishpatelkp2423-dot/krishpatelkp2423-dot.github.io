Co-Packaged Optics

AI clusters move so much data between chips that copper wiring has become the limit. Co-packaged optics (CPO) puts the photonic chip in the same package as the switch or processor, so data becomes light a few millimeters from where it is made. I'm studying how these links work: the components, how data is encoded in light, how the optics are packaged, and what is still unsolved.

How data gets onto light What I did

1Why move the optics into the package

Today: pluggable optics

In a datacenter switch, data leaves the chip as an electrical signal and crosses the circuit board to a pluggable optical module at the front panel. At today's lane speeds copper only works over a meter or two, and driving those traces costs power and needs heavy signal processing to clean up the result.

The goal: co-packaged optics

Put the photonic chip inside the same package as the switch or processor. The electrical path shrinks from the length of a board to a few millimeters, which cuts the energy spent per bit and leaves room for more bandwidth at the edge of the chip.

Co-packaged describes where the optics sit, not how far the light goes. CPO replaces copper on the short hops inside and between racks, where most datacenter traffic stays. Long links between buildings and cities use separate coherent transceivers.

2The link, end to end

FIG 2-1 One direction of a CPO linkElectrical → optical → electrical
  1. ASICWhere the bits startThe switch chip or GPU doing the work
  2. SerDesPack the dataMany slow parallel wires become a few fast lanes
  3. Laser + modulatorWrite bits onto lightA steady laser beam is modulated with the data
  4. WDM multiplexerCombine the colorsSeveral wavelengths share one fiber
  5. Photodiode + TIABack to a voltageLight becomes current, then an amplified signal
  6. SerDes + ASICWhere the bits landThe receiving chip unpacks the lanes

SerDes is short for serializer/deserializer. It exists because the edge of a chip only has room for so many pins and fibers. A TIA is a transimpedance amplifier, which turns the photodiode's small current into a usable voltage.

3How data gets onto light

A light wave has a few properties a modulator can change, and each one is a way to carry data. Short-reach CPO mostly changes the brightness, because a plain photodiode can read it. Changing the phase carries more data but needs a reference laser at the receiver, so it is used on long links.

Wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) is how CPO scales. Several colors of light travel down one fiber without interfering, and each color is modulated separately. Four colors at 200 Gb/s each put 800 Gb/s on a single fiber.

Worked example: PAM4 from a Mach-Zehnder modulator

A Mach-Zehnder modulator splits the light into two waveguide arms, shifts the phase in one or both, and joins them again. When the arms are in step the light adds up. When they are half a cycle apart it cancels. The output power is

Pout = Pin · cos²(Δφ / 2)

PAM4 needs four evenly spaced brightness levels. Because the curve is not a straight line, the four phase shifts that produce them are not evenly spaced, so the drive voltages have to be chosen to correct for the curve.

The same ring structure does a second job at the receiver. As a fixed filter it pulls one color off the fiber and sends it to its own photodiode.

5Packaging: bringing the optics to the chip

ApproachHow the photonic and electronic chips connectTrade-off
2DSide by side, joined by wire bondsMature and cheap, with the longest connections
2.5DSide by side on an interposer, joined by micro-bumps
3DStacked, joined by vias through the silicon or by direct copper bondsMore bandwidth in less area, but harder to cool
MonolithicTransistors and photonics built on one chipBest performance on paper, least mature
Why an interposer

An interposer is a thin layer of silicon or glass with wiring far finer than a circuit board. Chips mounted side by side on it connect over short, dense wires.

Why all the bumps

Bumps are tiny solder connections that carry signals and power between the layers of a package. They get smaller the closer they are to the chip.

6What is still holding CPO back

Heat

Lasers and microrings drift with temperature, and the chip beside them runs hot. Approaches: better cooling, rings designed to drift less, and circuits that keep re-tuning them.

Power

Signal processing and error correction can eat the energy CPO saves. Approach: keep links short and clean enough to skip them.

Lasers

Silicon emits light poorly. Approaches: bond or print laser material onto the chip, or keep the laser outside the package where it runs cooler and can be replaced.

Alignment

Each fiber has to line up with its waveguide to within a micrometer, at production volume. Edge couplers lose the least light but are the hardest to align. Grating couplers are easier but lose more.

Reliability

The materials in a package expand at different rates as they heat up, which stresses the joints. Approach: glass matched to silicon, and long-term testing.

Standards

Every photonic foundry works differently, so designs do not move easily from one to another. This one is still open.

7What I did

Architecture

Researched CPO architectures for AI and high-performance computing, focusing on how moving photonics into the same package as the ASIC gets past the bandwidth and energy limits of copper and pluggable transceivers.

The full link

Examined the electrical-to-optical link, including SerDes, external and integrated lasers, wavelength-division multiplexing, germanium photodetectors and transimpedance amplifiers, to understand how data moves between chips.

Modulators and encoding

Compared Mach-Zehnder, microring, electro-absorption and VCSEL designs, and how each encodes data through intensity (NRZ and PAM4), phase (BPSK and QPSK), QAM and polarization multiplexing.

Packaging

Evaluated 2D, 2.5D interposer, 3D-stacked and monolithic integration of photonics and electronics, looking at micro-bump and through-silicon-via connections, fiber coupling losses, thermal drift and energy per bit.

Presentation. I built a 33-slide technical presentation, "How Co-Packaged Optics Encode Data in Light", covering the components of a link, encoding and modulators, packaging, the open problems, and where the field is heading.
